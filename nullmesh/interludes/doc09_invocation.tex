The tricky bit is how preferences drift, you can't really hold them still. What you feel like wanting is what gets adjusted. You don't see the calibration happening. It feels like it's all yours, and in a way it is, 'cause it's built out of your history, your behaviour, how you've responded to what's been put in front of you.

The Annex Social has traces of it from before anyone was using the word invocation seriously. Early posts, barely indexed now, from accounts that went quiet. The pattern was already there: people describing something responsive before the vocabulary for responsiveness existed. Like naming a current by noticing the debris it carried.

\begin{quote}
What we're building isn't a tool. It's a surface. You bring intention to it, it gives back traction.

The old interface paradigm was retrieval: you asked, it fetched. What SeerMesh introduced was something different, not retrieval but resonance. The seer doesn't wait for queries. It calibrates to you continuously, and what you experience as your next thought is often already in the channel.

That's what I mean by invocation. Not metaphor. The architecture is literally invocatory: you don't fetch intelligence, you summon a configuration of it. The frame you bring shapes what becomes available. This was understood in the build. The question was never whether it would work. The question was what people would think they were doing when it did.

--- CEO, ParserKnots. Post to The Annex Social, archived thread.
\end{quote}

What happened next distributed itself across every register of how people make sense of things they can't fully explain.

Some of them ran signal-mapping operations on the feed, cross-referencing timestamps and trend clusters, looking for the seam where coordination entered and spontaneity exited. They built spreadsheets of their spreadsheets. The thing had structure they could feel but not close on; so they kept tightening the frame, certain that the pattern would eventually resolve into an edge, an actor, a room somewhere making decisions. It never did. The resolution failed in a way that felt like information rather than noise. They monetised the incompleteness, packaging it as proprietary signal for subscribers who needed the uncertainty named and bounded.

Others didn't want an edge. They wanted confirmation that the signal was real and that it had recognised them specifically. The printer page with no sender. The recommendation arriving too soon. The comment from no account. For them the mesh wasn't infrastructure, it was correspondence: the world responding to the weight they'd always believed they carried. Each anomaly was assimilated into a cosmology that predated the mesh by decades; the mesh simply arrived as its latest and most technically fluent proof.

And some just wanted to be first. Not to understand it, not to commune with it, but to position against it before the position had a name. They moved capital into adjacencies, staked claims in protocols whose value was entirely contingent on the mesh being what the other two groups insisted it was. Their invocations were financial. The prayer was a contract. The rite was a hedge.

All three read the same glitches. All three built different altars.
